Frequently asked questions

Short answers about The Slingshot: what it is, what it costs (nothing), and how it works in teaching. The educators page has the detail.

For players

What is The Slingshot?

A free, open-source game in which you found and run an AI startup in the UK. You play quarter by quarter across four years, racing rival firms to three milestones before the money or the time runs out. It runs in the browser; there is nothing to install and no account to make.

Is it really free?

Yes. Playing costs nothing, and there is no premium tier, no account and no catch. The game is an open, not-for-profit educational project.

How long does a session take?

Twenty-five to forty minutes for a full run. Returning players are quicker.

Is it fun?

Yes. It was designed by a gamer for gamers: a game first, an educational simulation second, not built to be worthy and dull. It is hard, and the difficulty is the point; some players have gone back thirty times chasing a better exit. As one Master's student put it, “it was a fun and motivating way to experience how business decisions are made under pressure.”

Where should I start?

Start in Seed mode with any venture that catches your eye, or build your own with Ada. Play your first run to learn the ropes rather than to win, read the journey report, then move to Core, where the competition is live. The how-to-play page covers the systems.

How hard is it?

Hard. Most first runs end with the money gone or a milestone missed, and the game does not soften the landing. Seed mode gives you more money and attention while you learn; Core is the standard game; Extreme removes the guide rails. The difficulty is the point: the tenth run wins because of what the first nine taught.

What should I focus on?

The next milestone. Each one asks for particular capabilities by a deadline, so read its requirements early and point your attention there. Keep one eye on cash: raising money takes time, and the game ends the quarter you cannot pay. Markets, hiring and events all serve those two clocks.

What decisions will I face?

The ones founders face. Whether to publish your research or protect it, which market to back and when to pivot, whose money to take and what it costs in equity, who to hire on a payroll you can barely cover, and what to do when your AI does something you did not ask for. There are nearly six hundred decision situations in the pool, drawn from real startup experience, and no run sees more than a fraction of them.

What will I learn?

The terms and concepts of technology venturing, picked up by using them: runway, dilution, term sheets, pivots, milestones. The game is practice before plunging in, built to grow your confidence and understanding of how ventures work. No technical knowledge of AI is required.

What mistakes do players often make?

Players tend to make the same mistakes. Doing whatever the funders suggest instead of thinking for yourself. Taking the first offer on everything, when staff, funders and partners all expect a negotiation. Never hiring, so the founding team ends up carrying the whole company. Spreading attention everywhere and committing to nothing. Ignoring grant funding, which is money without giving up equity. Never opening Market Opportunities, so a pivot costs full price. And picking a venture, location and milestone path that do not match your ambition. The game rewards deciding what you want and setting the company up for it.

What happens when my company fails?

You get the journey report, a PDF case study of your run: what you chose, what it cost, how it ended. Read it, take it to Schumpeter for the debrief, then play again with a different founder, venture or plan. The question worth asking between runs is how much was judgement and how much was luck.

Do I play against others?

Inside every run, rival firms race you to the same milestones, raise money and take your customers. Between runs, Core mode's Trajectory benchmarks your result against everyone who has recorded one, and a class can play a shared challenge from the same starting position.

Can I get help?

Yes. The player assistant is a free tool for players: ask it how a mechanic works, what a term means, or how to think about your next move, and it answers in plain language, grounded in the game's own materials.

Can I suggest features or improvements?

Yes, please. The Slingshot is a community project and the game is for everyone; players' ideas have shaped every version so far. If something confused you, broke, or ought to exist, email ammon.salter@wbs.ac.uk. Your experience and ideas can shape the next version of the game.

What are the mini-games?

Three moments you play rather than choose from a menu: working your booth at a conference, delivering a thirty-second pitch to investors in London, and closing deals under time pressure at demo day in Manchester.

What is Ada?

Ada builds a venture from your own idea. A short conversation settles the idea, the market, a company name and a location, and Ada saves a small file the game loads. You then play your own startup rather than one of the twelve built-in ventures. Meet Ada.

What is the Schumpeter debrief?

Every run ends with a journey report, a PDF recording your venture's history. Upload it to Schumpeter and talk the run through: what you chose, what it cost, and what you would do differently.

Is it accessible?

Yes. The game is developed against WCAG 2.2 AA, playable end to end by keyboard, and works with screen readers. If you hit a barrier, email ammon.salter@wbs.ac.uk and we will fix it.

Who built it?

The Slingshot was created by Ammon Salter (Warwick Business School), with Stefano Baruffaldi (Politecnico di Milano) and Federico Bignone (Warwick Business School), using AI-assisted development tools rather than a software studio. It is an open, not-for-profit educational project, built and maintained by a small team alongside their other work, and licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.

For educators

What do students need?

A modern web browser. Nothing to install, nothing to pay, no accounts to create. The game sets no cookies and holds no student data; the only things that leave the browser are anonymous, aggregate play counts and an optional, opt-in leaderboard.

What courses and levels does it suit?

Entrepreneurship, entrepreneurial strategy, innovation management, technology venturing and technology commercialisation, at undergraduate, postgraduate and executive levels. The three modes (Seed, Core, Extreme) let you match difficulty to the cohort.

What do students learn?

Entrepreneurial strategy in a nascent industry, where markets, categories and rules are still forming and there is rarely a single right answer; strategic decision-making under uncertainty; entrepreneurial finance, including runway, dilution, term sheets and non-dilutive grants; technology venturing and commercialisation, turning advanced technology into a business; university-industry interaction, engaging the science base that sits upstream of a deep-tech venture, deciding whether to publish or protect, and building university partnerships; managing investors, regulators, customers and partners; and learning through failure and structured reflection.

How does it compare with other entrepreneurship simulations?

Commercial simulations are typically licensed per student, need accounts and setup, and are often built around US examples and longer timetables. The Slingshot is free and open source, runs in the browser in twenty-five to forty minutes with no setup and no student accounts, is grounded in published research on AI ventures and the UK ecosystem, and comes with a free Educators Pack. Because the licence is open, you can adapt the game to your course rather than fitting your course to the game. Short free runs also make it replayable — many students play repeatedly, unprompted — and open access gives it uses a licensed product cannot match: a link shared for self-directed play, homework before class, an in-class tournament, or reflective assessment. Educators should judge any simulation against their cohort and learning objectives; those attributes are what The Slingshot offers.

Is the game still being developed?

Yes, continuously. Each version adds features: Version 2 brought Seed mode, competitive play through Trajectory, the Market Opportunity Navigator and token spending; Version 2.1 added Ada, four new sectors, the Schumpeter debrief, rebuilt mini-games, accessibility throughout and time-stamped play for assessment. The aim is a free resource that rivals anything in the commercial market, and is more fun to play.

Is it based on research?

Yes, in both its theory and its content. The game builds on the attention-based view of the firm (Ocasio, 1997): attention is the scarce resource, and the game's central tension is allocating it between competing goals, building internal capability while engaging the outside world that holds the resources a venture needs. That tension is also the question of the ESRC-funded project behind the game, Profiting from Science (ES/X003949/1), which studies how AI ventures form, grow and engage the science base. Its market-entry system embeds the Where to Play framework (Gruber and Tal, 2017), and its 590+ events draw on the literature on technology venturing.

Can I use it for assessment?

Yes, through reflection rather than results. Game outcomes make a poor measure of learning, because the game has a strong degree of randomness by design and a result reflects luck as well as judgement. What the design supports is a reflective essay: every run ends with a report, a PDF the student keeps, recording the venture's history, and students write about the choices they made during the playthrough, why they made them, and what they would do differently. That reflection is naturally the student's own. Only the student knows why they chose as they did, and the game's detailed mechanics are deliberately hidden, so the insight in a good essay comes from having played and thought, not from anything that can be looked up. The Educators Pack includes assignment ideas built exactly this way. Every report also carries a time-stamped frank: a unique run code, the time played and the pace of decisions, so a submitted report can be checked as the student's own run.

Can students play in teams?

Yes. A team of two to four can share one venture and make every decision jointly, which turns each quarter into a strategic argument, over hiring, funding, markets and pivots, of the kind a real founding team has. A class can also run a shared challenge: everyone starts from the same conditions and races to build the next AI unicorn, the game's ultimate prize, with a shared join code making the cohort its own anonymous leaderboard.

Can students play competitively?

Yes, in three senses. Inside the game every venture races three named rival firms in its sector. Between players, Core mode includes live competitive benchmarking, so students can compare their run against their class and against everyone who has played, and educators can run zero-cost in-class tournaments with no registration and no personal data captured. And players can compare themselves against themselves: benchmarking across their own earlier runs makes improvement visible from one replay to the next. Seed switches benchmarking off so beginners can learn without it.

How do I get the Educators Pack?

Email ammon.salter@wbs.ac.uk. It is free. The pack includes an educator AI assistant: ask it how to fit the game to your course, module or assignment, and it answers from the game's own materials.

What is the Trajectory Wizard?

A class-reporting tool in the pack. Pool your class's end-of-game reports, attach the Wizard's instruction file to an LLM, and get back a plain slide deck comparing the class, with draft questions for the debrief. Every number traces back to the reports.

Can I pair the game with real company examples?

Yes. The pack includes short mini-cases of real UK AI startups — Darktrace, Sylvera, Robin AI, Multiverse, Synthesia, Lendable, Wayve and Isomorphic Labs — each pairing with a sector, so students play the decisions and then read how the real company faced the same ground.
